# Round 3 — externally verified facts

Every checkable number in this round that comes from outside the repo is verified **here**, once,
against the primary reporting — not taken on trust from a demand file. A research file is not a
source: round 1 had an English writer fetch a source PDF and correctly refuse two figures its own
research file had asserted.

Writers quote **from this file**. If a fact is not here, it is not established, and the article
works around it rather than around the gap.

## The Malaysian "90% fake honey" story (February 2024)

This is the factual spine of the `is honey in malaysia fake` family. Verified 2026-08-25 against
Free Malaysia Today and cross-checked against Malay Mail, Borneo Post, Yahoo News Malaysia,
Business Today, SAYS and MustShareNews. Malay Mail returns HTTP 403 to automated fetch; its
substance is confirmed by the syndications.

### The claim

- **Who:** **Dr Zulkifli Mustafa**, senior lecturer in the **Neuroscience Department**, School of
  Medical Sciences, **Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM)**.
- **What:** that **90 per cent** of Malaysia's honey market — **explicitly including stingless bee
  (kelulut) honey** — consists of fake product.
- **Where it surfaced:** a **Berita Harian** report, **February 2024**.

### The government response — two ministries, two different things

- **Domestic Trade and Cost of Living (KPDN).** Minister **Datuk Armizan Mohd Ali**, 13 Feb 2024,
  directed the ministry's enforcement division to work with USM to obtain further information.
  He did **not** confirm or deny the figure.
- **Health Ministry (MOH).** 14 Feb 2024, responding to the Berita Harian report:
  - **769 honey samples** taken from various brands **from 2016** to that date.
  - **45 samples — 5.85 per cent — did not comply.**
  - The standard they failed is **Regulation 130 of the Food Regulations 1985**, which requires
    honey to contain **at least 60% reducing sugars**, **no more than 10% sucrose**, and **no more
    than 80 mg/kg hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF)**.
  - Enforcement action was taken against the companies and products concerned.

### Why the two numbers are not actually in contradiction — this is the article

They measure different things, and this is the whole reason the piece exists:

- MOH measured **compositional compliance** against a written standard — a sugar profile and a
  heat/age marker — on samples it drew itself.
- The 90% figure is a **researcher's characterisation of a market**, on a broader and undefined
  reading of "fake" that takes in adulteration, mislabelling and origin claims, and that
  explicitly includes the kelulut segment.

"Fake" is doing a great deal of unexamined work between the two. That is the honest resolution, and
it is more useful to a reader than either number alone.

### Rules that bind every article using this material

1. **Attribute both numbers by name, every time.** The 90% belongs to Dr Zulkifli Mustafa of USM;
   the 5.85% / 769 / 45 belongs to the Health Ministry. Never state either as the article's own
   finding.
2. **Do not compute a derived figure.** "So 94% of honey is fine" is arithmetic the ministry did
   not publish and does not follow — 769 samples MOH chose is not the market.
3. **Do not say the claim was retracted or disproved.** As of this pull it was **disputed**, not
   withdrawn. No retraction has been reported.
4. **Do not imply SUBUR's jars were among the samples, or were tested at all.** They were not, and
   nothing about SUBUR's testing status is published anywhere.
5. **Do not turn the fact-check into a safety claim about competitors** ("so buy from a trusted
   seller like us"). That converts reporting into an advertising claim about other people's
   products, which is both a legal problem and the thing that would make the piece worthless.
6. **Statute citation — a narrow, deliberate exception to the round's ban.** `FACTS-COMMON.md`
   forbids statute numbers in body copy, because rounds 1 and 2 cited the *advertising* ceiling
   wrongly and invented a penalty figure. **Regulation 130 is a different thing**: it is a
   composition standard, it is what MOH actually said it tested against, and naming it is reporting
   rather than legal advice. So:
   - **Permitted, in this article family only:** "Regulation 130 of the Food Regulations 1985", and
     the three thresholds above, stated as what the standard requires.
   - **Still banned everywhere, including here:** sub-regulation 18(6), any penalty or fine figure,
     any sentence that tells the reader what the law permits *them* to do, and any suggestion that
     SUBUR is certified or verified under any of it.

### The one honest product connection — and its limit

**HMF is a heat and age marker.** It rises when honey is heated or stored long and warm, which is
why a maximum is written into the standard. SUBUR is sold as a **raw** blend rather than a
heat-treated one, so the *category* logic is relevant and may be explained.

**The limit is absolute: no SUBUR jar has a published HMF figure, or any published test result.**
An article may explain what HMF is and why the standard caps it. It may **not** say or imply that
SUBUR is below the cap, has been tested, or would pass. Explaining a standard is not the same as
claiming to meet it, and the sentence that blurs the two is the one that fails this round.

## Sidr / Yemeni honey — what may and may not be stated

Backing the `what is sidr honey` family. Verified 2026-08-25 across specialist and retail sources.
**Read the caution at the end before using any of it.**

### Established, safe to state

- **Sidr honey is monofloral honey from the nectar of the sidr tree, *Ziziphus spina-christi*** —
  called the jujube or lote tree in English, `bidara` in Malay, `السدر` in Arabic.
- Its most prized origin is **Hadhramaut in Yemen, and within it the Wadi Do'an valley** — "Do'ani"
  on a label is a claim about that valley specifically.
- It is also produced outside Yemen — **Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Kashmir** all sell sidr honey —
  so **"sidr" is a floral source, not a country**. This matters and is the exact same distinction
  the site already makes for manuka: **UMF is New Zealand's licensing scheme, MGO is not
  country-tied.** Keep the two explanations consistent.
- Sensory profile as consistently described: **dark amber to brown, thick, with a distinctly
  non-sugary, complex flavour**.
- The **sidr tree flowers in a short annual window**, and that short window plus a limited growing
  geography is the standard supply explanation for its price.

### Do NOT state

- **No specific bloom duration.** Sources contradict each other outright — 20 days, 40–60 days, and
  "twice a year, spring and autumn" all appear. Say "a short annual flowering window" and stop.
  A number here would be invented precision.
- **No price figure, in any currency.** The $150–$500/kg and "$500+ at auction" figures circulating
  come from sellers of the product, not from a neutral source, and this site publishes no price of
  its own. State that sidr sells at a large premium and leave it there.
- **No grading claim.** "AA+", "Royal", "Premium" are seller-assigned marketing grades with no
  governing body behind them, unlike UMF. If the topic comes up, that absence *is* the useful point.
- **No health, therapeutic or "biologically active substances" claim**, however many of the sources
  make one. Almost the entire sidr corpus online is health-claim material. It is all off limits.

### The non-negotiable honesty rule for this family

**SUBUR is not sidr honey**, and every article in this family says so plainly rather than letting a
reader infer otherwise from the domain it is published on.

SUBUR is a **ten-ingredient raw honeycomb blend** — a *khalta* — which is a different product class
from a monofloral honey, in the same way it is a different class from manuka, kelulut or tualang.
`types-of-honey-in-malaysia` already draws exactly this line against kelulut and tualang; this
family extends the settled house position to the honey the audience actually types.

The retailer, Berkat Madinah Store, does sell Yemeni honey **on its own separate site**. That is
the retailer's other stock, not SUBUR, and an article on suburhoney.com must not blur the two or
imply SUBUR's jar contains any sidr.

## Kelulut and tualang — the two Malaysian honeys, and the one thing everybody gets wrong

Backing the `madu kelulut vs madu tualang` article. Verified 2026-08-25.

### Kelulut (stingless bee honey)

- Produced by **stingless bees**, farmed in Malaysia — the species named in the literature are
  **_Heterotrigona itama_** and **_Geniotrigona thoracica_**.
- The bees are **kept in hives and farmed**, which is why kelulut is the Malaysian honey most likely
  to be a domestic agricultural product rather than a wild harvest.
- Flavour is consistently described as **sour-sweet and fruity**, not plainly sweet — the single
  most reliable way a reader tells it apart in the mouth.
- **Yield per colony is small**, and that scarcity is the standard explanation for its price.

### Tualang honey — named after a tree it does not come from

- Produced by **_Apis dorsata_**, the giant honeybee, which is **wild and migratory and cannot be
  farmed**.
- The bees build large open combs high in the **tualang tree, _Koompassia excelsa_** — an emergent
  rainforest tree that rises above the canopy and can exceed 80 m.
- **The honey is named after the tree the bees nest in, not the flowers they feed on.** It is a
  **multifloral jungle honey**, gathered from whatever is flowering across the forest.
- It is **harvested by climbers** working at height on wild colonies, which is the honest reason for
  its price and its inconsistency batch to batch.

**This is the article's best paragraph, so do not waste it:** sidr is named for its *nectar*
(*Ziziphus spina-christi*), manuka for its *nectar*, tualang for the *tree the hive sits in*, and
kelulut for the *bee*. Four honeys, four completely different naming logics — which is precisely why
comparing them by name tells a buyer almost nothing. SUBUR names itself after **none** of them,
because it is a blend rather than a honey type.

### Hard fence for this article

The kelulut and tualang corpus online is **overwhelmingly medicinal** — the ranking academic result
for tualang is literally a systematic review of medicinal activities, and Malaysian sellers lean on
"more nutritious", "antioxidant", "medicinal properties".

- **None of it may be used.** No antioxidant claim, no nutrient comparison, no absorption rate, no
  named condition, no "more nutritious than". Not even attributed — attributing a health claim on a
  food seller's own domain is still that food's advertising.
- **No price figures** for either honey. Explain *why* each is expensive (small yield; wild
  harvesting at height) without quoting a ringgit figure the site cannot stand behind.
- **Watch the banned list hard.** This subject pulls strongly toward `tradisional` and
  `turun-temurun`; wild-harvest and heritage framing is exactly where they appear.
- **SUBUR is neither of these**, and saying so is the point of the comparison, not a hedge to bury.

### Cross-check with what the site already publishes

`types-of-honey-in-malaysia` (English, live) already draws the product-class line against kelulut
and tualang. This article goes deeper on the two of them against each other rather than restating
that page's list, and the two must not converge — check the live English article before writing.

## What a Malaysian food label must legally carry

Backing the `how to read a honey label` family (EN + AR). Verified 2026-08-25 against ChemLinked's
Malaysia labelling reference and cross-checked against the USDA FAS revised-requirements report and
Malaysian food-compliance summaries.

**Required on a packaged food label sold in Malaysia, under the Food Regulations 1985:**

| Particular | Detail |
|---|---|
| Designation / description of the food | what the product actually is |
| **Ingredient list in descending order of proportion by weight** | water, food additives and added nutrients aside |
| **Allergen declaration** | ingredients known to cause hypersensitivity must be declared. The named list includes **nuts and peanuts**, cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oat), soybean, fish, milk (including lactose) and egg |
| Net weight or volume | of the package contents |
| Manufacturer / importer details and country of origin | importer details and country of origin apply to imported product |
| Best-before or expiry date | for the foods that require one |
| Nutrition information | for designated products only, not universally |

Malaysian food-compliance guidance additionally describes allergen statements being set in **bold
capitals at a minimum type size** (e.g. `CONTAINS NUTS`).

### How this is used, and its limits

- The **descending-order rule is already asserted on the live Arabic article**
  `mukawwinat-khaltat-al-asal-bil-aashab` ("الأكبر كمية أولاً"). It is correct — do not contradict
  it, and do not restate its ten-name walkthrough either.
- **The allergen rule is the honest, checkable payoff.** SUBUR contains ground **walnuts and
  almonds**, which are tree nuts and therefore fall inside the declared-allergen list. That the site
  names them openly is a real, verifiable, unglamorous trust signal — and it is a fact about the
  ingredient list the site already publishes, not a new claim.
- **Net weight: general rule only.** An article may say a Malaysian label is required to carry a net
  weight declaration. It may **not** state SUBUR's — see D26 in `DECISIONS.md`. Describing what
  labels must carry is not the same as publishing a figure for this jar.
- **No certification claim.** A halal logo's meaning may be explained as a general label feature.
  **SUBUR publishes no certification of any kind**, so no article may state, imply or hint that this
  product carries one.
- **Best-before:** the rule is "for the foods that require one". Do **not** assert whether honey
  specifically is required to carry one — that is a category determination nothing here establishes,
  and the Malay expiry article must work from what jars actually show rather than from an asserted
  legal duty.

**Sources:** `https://food.chemlinked.com/foodpedia/labeling-requirement-in-malaysia` (fetched and
read 2026-08-25) · `https://food.chemlinked.com/expert-article/how-to-label-food-allergen-information-for-the-malaysian-market` ·
USDA FAS, *Revised Malaysian Labeling Requirements Effective January 1* (MY2023-0015).

## Baking: substituting honey for sugar

Added 2026-08-25 **after the adversarial review**, because the English baking article shipped a
draft whose headline ratio was wrong — and it was wrong precisely because this section did not
exist. Every figure in that article is externally checkable and none of it had a verified home.

### The source rule, in the form the sources actually give it

The consensus across the baking corpus is stated **by volume**: replace **1 cup of granulated sugar
with about ¾ cup of honey**, add ¼ cup less of another liquid, and drop the oven temperature by
about 25°F.

### The conversion, with the arithmetic shown

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| 1 US cup honey | **340 g** |
| 1 US cup granulated sugar | **200 g** |
| ¾ cup honey | 0.75 × 340 = **255 g** |
| **Honey per 100 g of sugar** | 255 ÷ 200 × 100 = **≈ 128 g** |
| 25°F difference | **≈ 14°C** — so a 180°C bake goes to about 165°C |

### The trap this section exists to prevent

**The volume ratio is 0.81. The mass ratio is 1.28.** They point in opposite directions, and
converting one into the other by carrying the number across unchanged produces "80 g of honey per
100 g of sugar" — which is short by roughly the honey-to-sugar density ratio and bakes a
noticeably under-sweetened result.

Honey is about 17% water, so **by weight it is not sweeter than sugar**. The charts call for less
honey *by volume* because honey is far denser, not because a gram of it is sweeter. Any sentence
that justifies using **less honey by weight** on the grounds that "honey is sweeter spoon for
spoon" is mixing a volume observation into a mass instruction — spoon-for-spoon is volume.

**Rule for this and any future round: a ratio taken from a source in cups may never be published in
grams without the cup masses and the division written out, as above.**

**Sources** (fetched 2026-08-25): `https://cupsandgrams.com/ingredient/honey/` ·
`https://www.gramsinacup.com/honey/` · `https://www.thecalculatorsite.com/cooking/cups-grams.php`.
The ¾-cup substitution rule and the 25°F reduction are the consensus of the competitor corpus
recorded in `demand-en.md`; they are a widely published baking convention, not a measured claim
about SUBUR, and the article attributes them as such.
