# Round 5 — verified facts

Six topics, each shipped as a three-language group (en + ms + ar), 18 articles. Every externally
checkable number below is verified once here; article files quote from this list rather than from
a search result. None of it is a claim about SUBUR — every citation is what a named health body,
standards body or public database publishes, reported as such.

## G1 — honey and diabetes

- **American Diabetes Association / Mayo Clinic**: no metabolic advantage to substituting honey
  for table sugar in a diabetes eating plan — both raise blood glucose, and honey should be counted
  toward total carbohydrate intake like any other sugar.
  Source: <https://www.goodrx.com/conditions/diabetes/can-diabetics-eat-honey>,
  ADA position summarised in the same piece.
- Chemically: sucrose (table sugar) is glucose+fructose bonded together and is split by digestion;
  honey already contains free glucose and fructose. The two routes converge on the same absorbed
  sugars — this is a mechanism, not a claim about either being "healthier".
- Honey's glycemic index varies by floral source, roughly **35–87** depending on variety — there is
  no single GI for "honey". A commercial blend cannot be assigned a GI without lab testing SUBUR
  has not published, so no GI number is stated for SUBUR itself.

## G2 — honey for cough and cold

- **Cochrane systematic review** (Oduwole et al., updated 2018/2021 pub.5): honey **probably**
  relieves cough symptoms more than no treatment, diphenhydramine or placebo in **children over 12
  months**, evidence rated low-to-moderate quality; most trial data covers a single night of use.
  Source: <https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD007094.pub5/full>
- **Mayo Clinic** treats honey as a reasonable home remedy for cough in adults and children over
  one, alongside standard advice (fluids, rest). Source:
  <https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/cough/expert-answers/honey/faq-20058031>
- The **under-12-months rule is unrelated and absolute**: infant botulism risk, already the site's
  own published position on every honey-for-babies page. This article states it again rather than
  assuming the reader has seen it.
- Neither source claims honey cures, prevents or shortens a cold/flu illness — only cough-symptom
  relief, and only "probably"/"may". No article in this round upgrades that to a stronger verb.

## G3 — calories and sugar per serving

- **USDA FoodData Central**: 1 tablespoon of honey (~21 g) = **64 kcal**, 17.3 g carbohydrate,
  17.2 g sugar, 0.1 g protein, 0 g fat, 0 g fibre.
- 100 g honey ≈ **304 kcal** (same USDA ratio scaled).
- Comparison figure only, not a claim: 1 tablespoon granulated white sugar (~12.5 g) ≈ **48 kcal**,
  ~12.6 g sugar — honey has more mass and more calories per spoon because a tablespoon of it is a
  heavier, denser pour, not because it is "worse"; the two are simply not equal volumes of sugar.
- These are properties of honey as a substance, not of SUBUR's blend specifically — a nut-and-herb
  blend's exact per-serving numbers would need SUBUR's own nutrition panel, which is not published
  and is not invented here.

## G4 — honey vs white sugar

- Same GI comparison as G1: honey's floral-dependent GI (35–87) straddles white sugar's ~60–65;
  neither is reliably "lower" as a blanket rule.
- Composition difference that is true and citable: honey is roughly 17–20% water and carries trace
  amounts of enzymes, pollen and minerals that refined white sugar (pure sucrose) does not; none of
  this rises to a health claim under any of the sources reviewed, and none is stated as one here.
- **No source reviewed calls honey a healthier substitute for sugar in a general population.** The
  ADA's plain answer (G1) is the closest thing to a verdict, and it says the two are comparable for
  blood glucose. Anything sold as a decisive "honey wins" answer would be inventing a consensus that
  does not exist, so this article's job is describing the real difference, not declaring a winner.

## G5 — how much honey per day

- **WHO Guideline: Sugars intake for adults and children (2015)**: free sugars — including sugars
  naturally present in honey, syrups and fruit juice — should be under **10% of total energy
  intake** (strong recommendation), with a conditional recommendation to go further, under **5%**,
  for additional benefit. 5% of a 2,000 kcal diet ≈ **25 g, about 6 teaspoons**, of free sugar from
  ALL sources combined, not honey alone.
  Source: <https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/free-sugars-adults-ncds>
- **Malaysia**: MOH/WHO guidance cited by Gleneagles Malaysia gives the 10%-of-2,000-kcal figure as
  **50 g (~10 teaspoons) added sugar per day** for a healthy adult, with the same further 5%/6-tsp
  benchmark. Source: <https://gleneagles.com.my/health-digest/changing-your-sugar-habit-effectively>
- The number is a **total-diet ceiling for all added/free sugar**, not a honey dose or a SUBUR
  serving size. No article states "X tablespoons of SUBUR is safe" — that would invent a
  product-specific allowance no source publishes.

## G6 — honey at suhoor (Ramadan)

- Nutrition-science baseline only, no religious or medical claim: honey is a simple sugar and
  digests quickly, producing a fast rise and fall in blood sugar; pairing a fast-acting sugar with
  protein, fat or fibre (yogurt, oats, nuts, wholegrain bread) slows absorption and extends the
  feeling of fullness through a fasting day — standard nutrition-science mechanics, not specific to
  honey or to fasting.
  General suhoor-nutrition framing consistent with hospital patient-education material, e.g.
  <https://www.asterhospitals.ae/health-library/newsdetail/best-foods-for-suhoor> (used for the
  general "pair simple sugar with protein/fibre" mechanic only — no specific figure or claim from
  that source is quoted).
- **Hydration**: fasting-day advice consistently recommends spreading fluid intake across the
  non-fasting hours rather than loading it at one meal; suhoor is one opportunity among several, not
  a hydration guarantee. Honey itself contributes negligible fluid.
- No source claims honey is required, superior to other suhoor foods, or has any special fasting
  benefit beyond being a food that supplies quick-acting carbohydrate. Framed here as a practical
  choice, not a prescription — and the word "tradition"/"traditional" is banned sitewide
  (`scripts/qa/banned-terms.mjs`), so this round earns the point through nutrition mechanics, never
  through a cultural-practice claim.

## What none of these six articles claims

No claim that honey, or SUBUR specifically, prevents, treats, cures, manages or reduces the risk of
diabetes, a cough, a cold, weight gain or any other condition — the Malaysia Food Regulations 1985
ceiling this whole site is written to. Every source above is reported as "X says Y", the way the
live `is-honey-safe-during-pregnancy` and `can-babies-have-honey` articles already do. SUBUR's own
identity — the ten named ingredients, no published price, no certification claim — is referenced
only where an article would otherwise leave a reader wondering whether it applies to SUBUR
specifically; it never repeats a claim as if made by any health authority.
