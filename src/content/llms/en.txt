## The product

SUBUR Honey (also written Subur Honey, Madu Subur, and in Arabic عسل سوبور) is a honey blend, not a plain honey. Its base is raw honeycomb - honey as it comes out of the hive, not a filtered, heat-treated syrup. Into that base go nine further ingredients, every one named on the label: saffron, ginseng root, star anise, fresh ginger, walnuts, nutmeg, cinnamon, black seed (habbatus sauda) and almonds. Ten in total, counting the comb. There is no "proprietary blend" wording on the jar and nothing withheld from the list.

Naming everything is the product's central idea. Malaysian households already buy most of these one at a time: habbatus sauda from the Arabic grocer, saffron brought back from umrah, ginseng from the Chinese medicine hall, cinnamon and star anise from the spice aisle, walnuts and almonds from the dried-fruit shelf. SUBUR puts them in one jar, held together by honey. A teaspoon is a habit anyone will actually keep; ten separate jars is not.

## The ten ingredients

What each ingredient is, where it is grown, how households actually cook and eat it, and why it earns a place in a blend built for couples. These are descriptions of a kitchen - what goes into the pot, when, and for whom. They are not statements about what the product does to the person eating it.

### 1. Saffron

The dried red stigma of the *Crocus sativus* flower - three threads per flower, picked by hand, which makes it by weight the most expensive spice in the world. Iran grows most of the world's supply, with Afghanistan, Kashmir, Morocco and Spain behind it.

Saffron has been in Persian, Arab, Mughal and Kashmiri kitchens for over two thousand years: the spice of weddings, of celebration rice, of the drink offered to a guest you want to honour. In Gulf and Persian homes a few threads go into hot milk or into the teapot at the end of a cold day, and saffron is the spice that turns up at a wedding and in the first cooking of a new household - exactly the register a blend for couples wants. In SUBUR it also signals a premium blend rather than cheap honey with flavouring.

### 2. Ginseng root

The fleshy, forked root of the *Panax* genus - Korean or Asian ginseng (*Panax ginseng*) from Korea, north-eastern China and eastern Russia, and American ginseng (*Panax quinquefolius*). The root sits four to six years in the ground before harvest, and old roots command startling prices at auction in Seoul and Hong Kong.

In Korean, Chinese and Malaysian-Chinese households ginseng is the root that goes into the pot when the weather turns and the week is long - simmered with chicken into soup, brewed as tea, a slice chewed on the way out of the door. Malaysia knows it well: the Chinese medicine halls of Petaling Street, Ipoh and Penang sell it in a dozen grades, and a Malaysian shopper does not need it explained. In the blend it is the second premium signal after saffron - one Arab-world anchor, one East Asian - and the bitter, resinous note that stops a jar of honey and nuts from tasting like a dessert.

### 3. Raw honeycomb - the base

The wax structure bees build and fill, harvested and used whole rather than spun, strained and pasteurised into a clear liquid. It carries the honey, traces of pollen and propolis, and the wax itself. Most honey on a Malaysian supermarket shelf has been filtered and warmed so it stays clear for years. Raw honeycomb has not.

Honey is one of the oldest foods in continuous human use and one of the few named directly in the Qur'an - a large part of why it carries the weight it does here. Across the Arab world, Malaysia and the subcontinent it has been the standard household daily food for general wellbeing for centuries: eaten by the spoon, stirred into warm water, given to guests. It is the base here for taste and keeping - honey carries strong spices, bitter seeds and ground nuts in a form people will happily eat every morning, without preservatives - and for trust: nobody feels they are "taking something", they are having a spoon of honey together.

### 4. Black seed (habbatus sauda)

The small, angular black seed of *Nigella sativa* - habbatus sauda in Malaysia, حبة البركة ("the seed of blessing") in Arabic, black cumin or kalonji in English. Grown in Egypt, Ethiopia, Turkey, Syria, India and Pakistan. Sharp, peppery, faintly bitter; nothing like cumin.

No ingredient in this jar carries more weight in a Muslim household. Black seed is named in the hadith, which is why a Malaysian or Arab shopper reads habbatus sauda on a label first. The seed has been a kitchen staple across the Arab world, Turkey, South Asia and the Malay archipelago for over a thousand years - whole seed stirred into honey, pressed into oil, scattered onto bread before baking, kept in the spice cupboard rather than the medicine cabinet. Black seed in honey is the original form of this whole category, and the mixture households here have long stirred together for themselves in a bowl; SUBUR starts from that pairing and builds the rest around it.

### 5. Walnuts

The wrinkled kernels of *Juglans regia*, the Persian walnut, native to the belt running from the Balkans through Iran and Central Asia. Iran, Turkey, China, Chile and California are the major producers today.

The walnut sits at the centre of Arab celebration food: baklava, ma'amoul, the walnut-and-honey plates put out at weddings and at Eid across the Levant, Turkey and Iran. Walnuts and honey together is a pairing so old in Arab, Persian, Turkish and Greek households that it is effectively one dish - the bowl pushed across the table towards a student, packed for a journey, or set out when guests arrive without warning. In the blend it does the plainest job of the ten: a teaspoon of honey alone is sugar, while a teaspoon carrying ground walnut is food.

### 6. Almonds

The kernels of *Prunus dulcis*, a stone-fruit tree originally from the Levant, Iran and Central Asia, now grown at scale in California, Spain, Australia and Iran. Botanically a seed rather than a nut - a cousin of the peach.

Almonds run through Arab, Persian, Turkish and Indian household food: soaked and peeled overnight and eaten first thing in the morning, ground into sweets and drinks, scattered over rice, served sugared at every wedding. In Indian and Pakistani homes the bowl of almonds left in water overnight is peeled at breakfast and put in front of children before school, and across the Gulf almonds and honey are what comes out when a guest sits down. In the blend they are the milder, sweeter partner to the walnut - where walnut is rich and slightly bitter, almond is clean and creamy - and, like walnuts and saffron, they are wedding food.

### 7. Fresh ginger

The rhizome of *Zingiber officinale*, a plant of tropical Asia grown across India, China, Indonesia, Thailand and Malaysia itself - halia is a staple of the Malaysian kitchen garden and wet market. Fresh ginger is sharper and more aromatic than the dried powder.

Ginger's household role here needs little explanation. Teh halia is a national drink; ginger goes into confinement food for new mothers, into the pot for anyone caught in the rain, into fish to cut the smell of the sea. Across Malay, Chinese, Indian and Arab households alike, ginger is what goes into the pot when someone is cold. In the jar it ties the blend to Malaysia, and it does real work: it lifts the honey, keeps the sweetness from cloying, and stops a spoonful of nut-and-honey paste from sitting heavy first thing in the morning.

### 8. Cinnamon

The inner bark of trees in the *Cinnamomum* genus, dried into quills. Ceylon cinnamon comes from Sri Lanka; the thicker, darker cassia most of Asia actually cooks with comes from Indonesia, southern China and Vietnam. In Malay it is kayu manis - "sweet wood", which is the whole description.

Cinnamon is in almost every Malaysian rice and curry dish that begins with whole spices, and in Arab and Turkish households it goes into coffee, rice, tea, and the warm milk given to someone at the end of a long day. Across the Arab world, Turkey, India and the Malay world a stick goes into the cup poured for a guest and into the warm drink made at the end of a long day. It makes the blend taste sweet without adding sugar, and it is the most universally liked of the ten: the flavour that turns a sharp black-seed-and-ginseng blend into something both husband and wife will happily take at six in the morning. In a product whose premise is that both keep taking it, that is not a decorative role.

### 9. Star anise

The dried, eight-pointed seed pod of *Illicium verum*, an evergreen native to southern China and northern Vietnam, where nearly all the world's crop is still grown. Liquorice-sweet, unmistakable, and far stronger than it looks - one pod flavours a whole pot.

Malaysians eat star anise constantly, often without naming it: as bunga lawang it is the backbone of nasi briyani, beef rendang, sup tulang, bak kut teh and every good curry powder from Penang to Johor. It is one of the five spices in Chinese five-spice powder, and across Chinese and Malay households a pod goes into the tea brewed after a heavy meal and into the warm drink made at the end of the day. In SUBUR it is the bridge spice: warm rather than sharp, it makes the ginger and black seed easier to take by the spoon and gives the blend an aroma that reads as domestic to a Malaysian nose - and a blend nobody enjoys is a blend nobody keeps taking.

### 10. Nutmeg

The seed kernel of *Myristica fragrans*. For centuries it grew nowhere on earth but the Banda Islands of Maluku, in what is now Indonesia; today it is also grown in Grenada, Sri Lanka, India and, closer to home, in Penang, where buah pala and its juice are a local institution.

In Malaysian and Indonesian households nutmeg is a working ingredient rather than an exotic one: nutmeg juice sold cold on Penang street corners, grated nutmeg in soup, in kuih and in the spice mix for meat. In Arab and Indian kitchens jauzi/jaiphal is used the same way and in the same tiny quantities - a scrape into warm milk at the end of the day, a pinch into the mince. It is a rounding spice: used sparingly it pulls cinnamon, star anise and ginger into one aroma instead of three competing ones, and gives the blend the resinous back-note that makes it taste finished.

## How couples take it

There is no course and no schedule. It is a morning habit, deliberately small enough that two busy people can keep it for years.

- **One teaspoon, on an empty stomach.** First thing after waking, before food, before coffee. A level teaspoon is the whole serving; more is not better.
- **Wait about fifteen minutes.** Then eat breakfast as normal - most households take the spoon, put the kettle on, and by the time the coffee is poured the wait is over.
- **Both partners, from the same jar.** The blend is identical for husband and wife. A 500g jar is sized for two people taking a teaspoon each daily; 400g suits one person; 250g is the jar to start with.
- **Every day, not occasionally.** Couples who keep it going tend to have attached it to something they already do without thinking: the kettle, the alarm, the first prayer of the day.
- **Never into boiling liquid.** Boiling a raw honey undoes the reason for using raw honeycomb. For a drink, let it cool to comfortably warm first.
- **Straight off the spoon is the normal way.** On bread, on oats, folded into yoghurt, or followed by a glass of warm water - all fine. Use a dry spoon: water in a raw honey jar is the one thing that will spoil it.

It is eaten the way Malaysian households have always eaten honey, black seed and almonds - as part of the morning. It is not a substitute for medical care, and anyone with a health concern should speak to a doctor.

## Authenticity and trust

**Buy from the store, not from a listing.** The most reliable authenticity check is the channel: a jar from a Berkat Madinah branch or one of the store's own online channels is genuine; a jar from anywhere else is one nobody can vouch for.

**Read the label.** A genuine jar names all ten ingredients and prints the net weight with its ±5% tolerance. A label hiding behind "natural blend" or "herbal mixture" is not this product.

**Expect crystallisation.** Raw honey granulates and firms up in the jar. Cheap commercial honey often does not, because it has been heated and micro-filtered to sit clear on a shelf for two years, so crystallisation is one of the better everyday indicators that you bought raw honey rather than syrup. Soften a firm jar by standing it in warm - never boiling - water. Do not microwave it.

**Expect texture and taste.** SUBUR should not pour thin and clear; it is thick and granular with visible ground nut and spice, and it should taste of its ingredients - sharp black seed, warm ginger and cinnamon, the bitterness of ginseng, the perfume of saffron. A blend that tastes only of sweetness is mostly sugar. Syrup-cut "honey" is a well-documented problem in this market, which is exactly why the brand names every ingredient and names its only seller.

**What is not claimed.** No certification number, registration number, award, rating, review count or founding date is asserted anywhere on this site. What is stated is what can be checked: the ingredients, the jar sizes, the two branch addresses, and the fact that one store sells it.
